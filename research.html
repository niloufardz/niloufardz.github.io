Getting usable power out of very small sources

Advanced High-Gain and Fault-Tolerant Electronic Interfaces for Small-Scale Energy Harvesting Systems

Sensors and smart devices increasingly run on energy harvested from their surroundings, such as sunlight or heat. These sources give low, variable voltages that a circuit cannot use directly. My thesis develops power-electronic interfaces that raise this voltage with as few actively controlled switches as possible, extract the available energy, and keep working after a switch fails.

Supervisors: Prof. Philippe Poure and Prof. Slavisa Jovanovic.

thesis/ · contributions3 parts
01 Two-stage interfaces for energy harvestingBuck · Boost · Buck–Boost · Ćuk · SEPIC → 25 architectures In a two-stage interface, the first converter extracts the maximum power from the source (MPPT) and the second regulates the output voltage. Combining five basic converters gives twenty-five possible architectures, which I compared. The cascaded Boost–Boost was chosen as the main case study: it offers one of the highest voltage gains, its continuous input current suits MPPT, and it needs few components. ICECS '24
02 High-gain single-switch convertersSSQB · VL-QBC · DVL-QBC · QVL-QBC · unified method Replacing one of the two switches of the cascaded Boost with a diode gives the single-switch quadratic Boost (SSQB), which keeps the high gain while needing only one switch to drive. Adding voltage-lift cells produced three new topologies; at 5 V input, 50 kHz and 50 % duty cycle they reach voltage gains of about 4.7, 8.6 and 14, confirmed on a reconfigurable prototype. I then generalized this into a unified method for turning any two-stage converter into a single-switch equivalent. Energies '25
03 Fault-tolerant cascaded Boost–Boostopen-circuit switch faults · service continuity If a switch fails open, a converter normally stops. Using the same single-switch idea, I designed fault-tolerant versions of the cascaded Boost–Boost in which one shared backup switching path takes over after a fault in either switch, so energy conversion and output-voltage regulation continue. The simplified version needs only one extra switch and one diode. All structures were validated by analysis, simulation and 5 V, 5 W experiments. faults

Interactive lab: why a quadratic boost?

A standard boost converter has a gain of 1/(1−D). To reach high gain it needs a duty cycle close to 1, where losses explode. A quadratic boost squares that gain, so the same output needs a much smaller duty cycle. Move the slider and watch the current loops.
